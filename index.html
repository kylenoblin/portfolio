Beat Prime with the stores we already had.

The mandate: capture same-day delivery demand without building dedicated infrastructure, and go head to head with Amazon Prime. The asset nobody was using that way was 2,000+ stores sitting near almost every customer.

We built fulfillment visibility straight into the cart: real-time inventory across every store, delivery-zone math, and dynamic pricing by distance and window. The cart showed Standard, Store Pickup, and Express side by side. Express read “Get it today by 6pm,” not “Express Delivery,” because a specific time creates urgency that a category label never will.

Four teams, one story.

SMB growth was flat, and it wasn't a messaging problem. It was structural: product, sales, marketing, and care each expressed a different definition of value, and customers felt the chaos.

Three paths were on the table: a campaign-only fix (fast, but it just widens the gap between promise and reality), a pricing play (short-term, and it reinforces the commodity trap), or re-architecting the whole experience around one value system. I chose the slowest, hardest one, "Make It Personal," and drove it into packaging, sales framing, and industry-specific narratives for F&B, Healthcare, and Retail.

$7M

A $7M fix, one screen at a time.

Too many customers couldn't finish self-install on their own, and every failure turned into a truck roll, meaning a technician dispatched to the customer’s address, plus a call, a chat, sometimes a cancellation, all avoidable cost, and a rough first impression of the brand.

I redesigned the flow so a non-technical customer could actually complete it: real-time connection status, adaptive troubleshooting that reacts to what's failing, and guided setup that never assumes expertise. Failed installs dropped from 30% to 3%.

Where the ~$7M/year comes from
~50,000 failed installs/yr×$150 per truck roll×90% of failures removed = ~$7M/yr

Journey orchestration
9 → 3

No one owned the Becoming journey, so I did.

Cox ranked 9th out of 10 nationally in J.D. Power. Every business unit blamed another, and no one owned the system a new customer actually moved through. One customer put it plainly: “Every step felt like starting over with a new team.”

The easy path was patching handoffs one at a time, which fails systemically. Instead I stood up "Becoming a Customer" as a named program with one owner and one set of outcomes across Product, Sales, Support, Field Operations, and Technology. We mapped the 120-day journey and used Moment Architecture to invest only in the moments that actually move churn and satisfaction. It took four months just to change field-tech comp from install speed to include retention.

Building the practice

From optional agency to owning the journey.

When I arrived, design was four production designers with no real UX pedigree, run as an optional in-house agency. Nothing was assigned to us. We fought for the work, and when we won it we partnered, designed, shipped, and that was the end of it. No ownership of what happened next.

We earned a seat by changing what we talked about. Not screens, but what the screens drove. A setup flow that cut failed installs was an OpEx line. An experience that moved satisfaction showed up in add-on sales. Once the work was framed in hard numbers with the data behind it, design stopped being a service getting evaluated and became a business argument. That's when we got into the rooms.

Then we changed the model to match. We moved from a center of excellence to embedded and matrixed so we'd own journey experiences instead of handing off deliverables, and I sat on the leadership steering committee that reorganized Cox around customer journeys, which is what made that ownership real. That wasn't a change any one person made.

The bar moved with it. Screen-level design gave way to research as a standard practice, service design as a discipline other orgs now ask for by name, and a team that works in the messy middle connecting product, marketing, care, and operations. Of everything I installed, service design as a practice is the one that mattered most. It's what grew the team from four to fifteen, twenty counting contractors.

The practice spread past the team. We trained every new hire and every hiring manager in human-centered design, and then departments started coming to us asking for their own teams to go through it. Thousands of people across Cox have now been trained on it.

The thinking underneath

Three frameworks Cox still runs on.

Moment Architecture

Where to invest, and where not to

Not every interaction deserves equal investment. A way to decide where to prevent failure, where to exceed expectations, and how to get cross-functional teams to agree on it. It became the standard at Cox for evaluating experience work across Product, Marketing, Operations, and Technology.

Experience IQ

The orchestration engine

The layer that connects why we build (principles), what we build (pillars and capabilities), and how it ships (enablers and UI), so every interaction traces back to a business outcome instead of a hunch.

The Trace

Every screen traceable to strategy

Moment Architecture decides where to invest. Experience IQ decides how to build it. The Trace is the audit: it runs a single line of interface copy all the way up to the business outcome it serves, then back down again. If a screen can't be traced in both directions, it isn't ready.
